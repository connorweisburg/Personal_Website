On progress trackers

The first big problem I identified within the continuity program at Metro was a lack of progress tracking. Nobody knew the state of all of the plans! There was no way to monitor the program and gauge its status without having to go into all of the plans themselves. This caused lots of confusion, headache, and inconsistencies across plans because that information is too complex to be held in one head. Tasks were slipping through the cracks left and right.

I needed a central source of knowledge about what year each was updated most recently, what the update had looked like, whether it was still pending, how finished it was.

In response to this, I designed a progress tracker in a spreadsheet that listed all plans, who is involved, status (not started, in progress, completed, - which eventually turned into a percentage), and notes that listed recent tasks and other significant information. Now in just one screen, it was possible to analyze the whole program.

This sped things up. From a bird’s eye view, you can see the next “worm”. From a progress tracking view, you can see the next email to write. Quickly, without having to dig around in the dirt.

Maintenance Schedules, an unexpected lifesaver

One key contribution I made to Metro's continuity project is organizing the project into a Gantt chart. I was reading a book about production flows that mentioned Gantt charts, I did some research, and immeidately knew it would be helpful. For those who don't know, Gantt charts help plot out time in projects. The main idea is that it's easier to take one bite out of the elephant at a time rather than try and stuff it down all together. If you've got a huge project, you have to break it down into smaller, more manageable chucks (tasks). A Gantt chart plots those tasks out over time with some stagger so that work remains fairly constant.

For my work on Metro's continuity plans, I experienced a lot of confustion. There were fairly well-seperated tasks in the form of 21 separate plans that had to be updated regularly, but I was often confused about when I should start each plan. I noticed deadlines were frequently tied to audit requirements because the plans weren't being updated frequently enough. There hadn't been a full, finished update on all plans in almost 10 years. Additionally, some of my collaborators in other departments asked for deadlines (to keep them accountable), and each time, I had to make a decision off the cuff. "Oh lets say we'll try to get it all done in a month or so". It felt aribitrary, and I think the looseness made it easy to push past suggested deadlines, which made some plans take way longer than expected. I'll be honest, without a clear framework, I also pushed deadlines.

That's where the Gantt chart comes to the rescue. With 21 plans that needed to be updated every two years, it fit so well into month-long blocks. If a plan is started every month with a deadline three months later (which is very reasonable), all plans would be compeleted when they needed to be! The work is spread out evenly over the entire 2 year timespan, it gives an easy to follow time structure to everyone involved, and it reduces the number of decisions made off the cuff during the planning process. Now every plan could be updated like this:

2 weeks before the end of a calendar month, reach out to stakeholders to schedule a meeting in the first week of the month. Tell everyone in the first meeting that updates will be due in 3 months. Explain that this would keep grease in the wheels and would keep the whole program on track to meet that biannual update requirement. If it takes longer, the gears don't get the grease when it's needed, and the whole system slows down and backs up. Inspire them to do their part to keep everything moving according to this schedule. Set up a couple of check-ins the first week of every month during the 3 month process, with the 4th meeting being the deadline. Viola! An easy-to-follow system that gets the work done on time.

The magic of the Gantt chart itself is that it visualizes this time structure in a way even my boss could understand and get behind.

Scripts!

I was very new to the professional game when I arrived at Metro. Even though I had a degree, a few years of random work experience, and an unusual professional development experience at my residency at the Oakley Art House, I was vastly inexperienced in the world of corporate America. I had no idea how to run a meeting, how to advocate for my area of assignment, or even how to string two sentences together in front of an Executive with 30 years of experience in their field. At the beginning, it was obvious that my boss trusted me even less than I trusted myself (as my ego frequently tried to outshine my skills… to my detriment). I was quickly getting up to speed on the knowledge base of this field so new to me, continuity planning, but I still struggled on my presentation of the topic in meetings. More than once, I felt the stares of powerful people that beamed into my soul 'oh look, another clueless consultant. Who hired this guy?'

I originally resented my boss for pressing me so hard on knowing my material, though I look back now with more appreciation. She HAD to make sure I knew what I was doing and saying before I opened my mouth because she had seen the garbage that poured out when I was on the spot. Over time, however, I was noticing key phrases that my boss used to introduce our meetings, finding a few sentences in the continuity literature that presented the topic clearly, and exploring new ways to explain some of the more technical aspects. That’s where I started writing scripts. I needed to know what to say before saying it. It seems obvious, but I had to really work at it. I had to make presentations that never saw an audience. I had to wander the halls, gesturing madly and reciting lines to make sure the flow felt natural. I had to polish paragraphs and post them at the bottom of my monitor to read off of during virtual meetings. I had to perform some of this for my boss in one on ones to make sure I was on track. It took a lot of time and focused effort.

When I was in college and diving into jazz standards to hone my technical musician skills, I had a pianist friend who was doing the same. He used to say to me “learn ‘em so you can forget ‘em”. He definitely was taking that maxim from one of the jazz greats, but that philosophy (about scripts instead of standards) is what got me to a place of confidence in meetings and conversations about business continuity. I had to put the work in to polish my phrasing and understanding so I could get to a point where I wasn’t looking at my written material and I could engage in the topic as it interacted with other people with different expertise. I no longer had to ‘try’ to remember certain phrases. Instead, the words flowed easily. I could identify where people were missing understanding and getting off topic, and I could guide them back to the core of the conversation. Through the use of scripts, I had paved my own way to clarity that others could follow.
